The AI build-out scoreboard

Five checkable tests of whether the AI build-out is real demand or malinvestment, plus the five physical tells that would show a slowdown before any share price does. Every figure is dated and sourced, and we update it every week — including the weeks when nothing moves.

What changed — as of 6 November 2026

This issue brings the page up to date with the third-quarter filings that landed between 22 and 30 October and the financing disclosures that followed them.

Test 1, savings or credit, got worse for the two biggest spenders. In the nine months to 30 September 2026, Amazon spent $175.7bn on property and equipment against $113.8bn of operating cash flow, and raised $115.1bn of long-term debt ($48.1bn of it in the third quarter); its long-term debt went from $65.6bn at December 2025 to $173.1bn26. Alphabet spent $132.0bn against $127.1bn of operating cash flow and, during 2026, issued $45.0bn of US-dollar and $35.8bn of foreign-currency senior unsecured notes, plus a $5.0bn secured term loan for Waymo in October at SOFR + 5.25%27. Meta ($83.1bn of capex against $96.9bn of operating cash flow)28 and Microsoft ($39.4bn against $58.0bn in the September quarter)29 still paid for capex out of operations in those periods, but both have since gone to the market. Microsoft sold $25.0bn of notes under an 8-K filed 4 November, its prospectus saying proceeds are for general corporate purposes that may include capital expenditures40. Meta, after $25.0bn of notes in May, completed $15.0bn of notes on 5 November, priced a further €9.0bn due to settle about 12 November, sold about $14.4bn of mandatory convertible preferred stock, and agreed forward sales of 46 million shares for which it receives no cash until it settles, by 19 November 202741. Alphabet also raised about $49.5bn of equity and preferred stock in June, and closed another €6bn of notes on 5 November42. Its free cash flow was −$9.1bn in the third quarter and −$5.9bn in the second. SpaceX signed a binding commitment on 24 October for a master finance lease on GPUs from an Apollo-managed lessor, with principal payments of about $40bn; it has not closed47.

Two cautions on reading those numbers. They are cumulative, and some borrowing may be pre-funding spending that has not happened yet, so they show who is borrowing, not that any of it is unsafe. And our June description, “hyperscaler core cash-funded”, was true when we wrote it and is no longer true of two of the four; the other two still cover capex from operations but are now raising outside money as well.

Test 2, rents, still rising. Silicon Data’s H100 index read $2.99 an hour on 6 November, up about 6.8% from $2.80 on 8 October30. GetDeploying’s provider median, at our last read on 6 November, is $3.88 an hour across 45 providers, up about 8% over 90 days and about 13% over 12 months35. They are different baskets, so the levels differ, but both point the same way. One caution: that median read $3.54 on 15 October and $3.85 on 26 October, and on 6 November alone we read $3.99 across 41 providers, $4.00 across 43 and $3.88 across 45. Which providers list a price clearly moves it, so we lean on the 90-day and 12-month changes rather than any single reading. A correction: we had dated the $3.39 median 19 August, which is the date of the article that carried it; the figures in that article are checked 12 September, and we have changed the date. A second correction: we labelled the $725bn capex figure February 2026; the February reporting we cited says nearly $700bn, so that label was wrong. We have replaced it with the latest company guidance, and source 21 records the change.

Test 3, circular financing, grew — and the guarantees now span several vendors. On 26 October Broadcom disclosed a residual-value backstop of up to about $43bn behind a special-purpose vehicle that takes on purchases of its AI racks and leases them to a customer it does not name; Broadcom calls it a contingent liability with “a low probability of occurring”31. Anthropic’s IPO filing, amended 2 November, shows the buyer’s side of that kind of structure: a five-year Broadcom equipment lease of about $125.2bn, and the right to issue up to $42.0bn of convertible notes to Broadcom to finance the lease, alongside $20.0bn of note capacity with Google and $15.0bn with Amazon32. The other side of that ledger, from the same filing: nothing was outstanding under those three facilities at 30 September, and the company says it is “not dependent” on the Broadcom capacity. Oracle went the other way on market borrowing: it capped new capital-markets funding for fiscal 2027–30 at $20bn and completed it on 4 November, while saying vendor financing, “customer-provided hardware and customer prepayments are expected to reduce upfront cash requirements”33. Reported but not signed: Nvidia in talks to guarantee debt for Nscale’s West Virginia campus34. Two more vendors surfaced in third-quarter filings. AMD’s guarantees of partners’ data-centre leases and debt reached a maximum of $8.5bn at 26 September, from $4.1bn at 27 June, alongside $13.3bn of leases it expects to assign or sublease and $6.2bn of compute commitments; it says no partner was in default and nothing had been paid36. Alphabet’s credit derivatives, which backstop data-centre payment obligations, had a maximum exposure of $48.0bn at 30 September, from $16.9bn at December37, and on 4 November Google agreed to backstop Anthropic’s obligations on TeraWulf leases tied to about $19bn of lease commitments38. Amazon put $50.0bn into OpenAI in the nine months to 30 September, $21.3bn of it in the third quarter, and $10.0bn into Anthropic39. Going the other way, Microsoft ended its agreement to anchor Nscale’s 50MW Loughton site, a single-site customer exit after grid officials told Nscale not to expect power until the 2030s46. The IMF now puts a figure on the pattern: fourteen disclosed deals since 2023, about $300bn of equity plus $650bn of debt, against $1.4tn of future revenue commitments, a tally on its own definition rather than ours45. A search by company and by filing found nothing further in filings, but private companies disclose little, so this list may still be incomplete.

What did not move. There was no new transformer lead-time survey. We searched the third-quarter 10-Qs of Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and Microsoft for a further shortening of server useful lives and found none. CoreWeave had not filed its third-quarter 10-Q by the end of 6 November; it reports results on 10 November, so its debt figure below is still the 30 June one. One planning series did move: Dodge’s Momentum Index fell 9.0% in October, led by weaker data-centre and warehouse planning, though Dodge notes data-centre activity swings month to month and we have no baseline of our own for it yet43.

Our read: Test 1 stays SPLIT, moving the wrong way, and the evidence for that direction is now stronger, because it no longer rests on one leveraged neocloud, and because all four hyperscalers have now raised outside capital this year. What would move it back: the two large borrowers stopping, or their operating cash flow catching their capex. What would move it further: a hyperscaler borrowing against the data centres themselves. Test 3 stays WEAKENING: more of the build-out now rests on sellers standing behind their own customers, with Broadcom, AMD and Alphabet alongside Nvidia, though no AMD guarantee had paid out at 26 September, Nvidia’s Ohio guarantee does not start until leases commence, the Anthropic notes had nothing outstanding at 30 September (Amazon’s facility fell from $20bn to $15bn after Anthropic took $5bn of it as equity), and Oracle has capped its market funding. What would move it back: those backstops shrinking as lease payments come in, or the notes staying undrawn. What would move it further: a backstop being called, or the vendor notes being drawn. Rents rising while capex accelerates is still the combination the slowdown tells would have to reverse.

Test 5 fired against our own expectation, and we are showing our working

We wrote this test's rule on 20 September, before looking at any of the data, because a test invented after you have seen the answer is not a test. The rule says the direction goes to WEAKENING if any of the five largest buyers shortens the useful life it assigns to its own servers. We also wrote down what we expected to find: STRENGTHENING, because the loudest available evidence pointed that way.

We were wrong. Amazon had already shortened, in a filing from February 2025, and said in plain language that AI was the reason. Our own rule marked the cell against the answer we anticipated.

Why this test exists. The big buyers have told their auditors these machines last six years, and that number is fixed in a filing for years at a time. The second-hand market re-prices every week. If demand is real, obsolete chips keep clearing near what the books say they are worth. If it is not, the resale price cracks first — before any income statement can show it, because the accounting cannot move that fast. What would move this cell back: the CoreWeave rebooking figures confirmed in a filing or transcript rather than trade press, and no further shortening in the next round of annual reports.

Why Test 4 is marked LIVE and never scored

Its measure is vibes. We track it because it moves money — it moved a great deal of money on 14 September — but we will not convert a mood into a score and then quote the score back at you as evidence. The other four tests have numbers with dates on them. This one does not, and pretending otherwise is how a framework stops being worth reading.

The five physical tells

What a real slowdown would look like

The AI build-out is a physical event. It needs transformers, switchgear, transmission, cooling, memory and optics, and those have lead times measured in years. A physical build-out slows in physical ways, and those show up in order books before they show up in anybody’s stock. These five are the tells. Most weeks, most of them will say no new print. Two of the five carry a figure in the table today; the other three sit in their own section below it, and one of those now has its first baseline.

Baselines we are still building

A tell with no baseline is not yet a measurement, so tells 3 to 5 sit here rather than in the table above, where an empty row still reads like a reading. We will add one source a month, published with its date and its basis, instead of inventing numbers to fill them.

  • 3. Grid interconnection queues — a slowdown would show as queues shrinking, projects withdrawing. Developers withdraw long before they stop announcing. No baseline carried: we have not published a queue figure of our own yet.
  • 4. Memory and optics pricing — a slowdown would show as pricing rolling over. These are the inputs with the least substitutability, so they crack late and they crack honestly. No baseline carried: we have not published a memory or optics price series yet.
  • 5. Data-centre starts — a slowdown would show as starts and permits declining: starts, not announcements. Announcements are free. First baseline: ConstructConnect recorded $9.4bn of US data-centre starts in September against a revised record $25.8bn in August, $135.8bn year to date. Dodge’s Momentum Index, which measures planning rather than starts, fell 9.0% in October to 260.4.43

What would change our mind

Our conclusion is own the bottleneck, not the boom. We would drop it if transformer lead times shortened materially from about 128 weeks while data-centre starts fell — that combination is a genuine slowdown in the physical chain, and it would mean the scarce inputs are no longer scarce. We would drop the other half of it, the caution on the leveraged middle, if GPU-backed paper started pricing tighter at the same rating and a syndication cleared without a concession. A sell-off in the shares of companies that sell into the build-out is neither of those things. Next full re-score: December 2026, on all five tests plus these five tells.

How to read this

Three states, and a lot of silence

  • STRENGTHENING / UNCHANGED / WEAKENING describe the underlying condition on that test since June — the evidence, not the share price and not our enthusiasm. Test 2 is STRENGTHENING because the demand evidence got better. Tests 1 and 3 are WEAKENING because the financing underneath got worse. They point in different directions on purpose: they do not cancel out, and a scoreboard that always points one way is a sales document.
  • LIVE means we track it but refuse to grade it. Only Test 4 carries it.
  • “No new print” is the most common cell on this page, and that is correct. Transformer lead-time surveys land roughly annually. Interconnection-queue studies are annual. Capex lands quarterly, with the filings. GPU rental indices refresh monthly at best. A weekly page built over sources that print monthly, quarterly or annually must mostly say that nothing happened — anything else means someone is refreshing a stale number to look busy. We would rather show you an old figure with an old date on it than a new-looking figure that is really the same one.
  • Silence is information. The thing this page exists to catch is the week when one of the five physical tells finally moves. You cannot see that week unless all the other weeks honestly say it did not.
