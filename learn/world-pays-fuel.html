Three Cents a Litre? What Iran’s Fuel Really Costs, and What the World Pays

A video doing the rounds shows a driver in Iran filling up for pocket change, with a caption saying Iranians pay 3 to 6 US cents a litre. That is true, but only for one of three prices. Most Iranian drivers pay under one cent a litre for most of their fuel, and in 2026 that nearly free fuel has come with queues and 20-litre limits. Below we explain Iran’s three prices, then compare what 28 countries and the EU as a whole paid for petrol and diesel before the war and in September 2026. We show why a litre costs under a cent in one country and more than $2.50 in another, and why the cheapest fuel is not always the most affordable.

The short version

  • The video is right about the top tier only. Since 8 September 2026, fuel bought beyond Iran’s monthly quota costs 100,000 rials a litre.1,2 That is 4.3 US cents at the open-market exchange rate (17 Sep), or 6.0 cents at the central bank’s official rate (16 Sep).3,4 The first 60 litres a month cost 15,000 rials: 0.65 cents. OUR MATH
  • Cheap is not the same as plentiful. Iran burns more gasoline than it refines. A US naval blockade since 14 July has choked the imports that filled the gap.5
  • The war raised pump prices almost everywhere, and diesel more than petrol. From late February to mid-September, US petrol rose 47% and US diesel 65% (23 Feb to 14 Sep).6 The EU average rose 26% and 35% (same dates).7
  • Before tax, a litre costs about the same in the US, the UK and the EU: roughly $1.00 to $1.26 (14 Sep). What separates the pump prices is mostly tax. Where tax is a large fixed amount per litre, it softened the shock in percentage terms. (Section 3 gives the workings.)
  • Cheap fuel is not the same as affordable fuel. A US worker on median pay earns a litre in about 2 minutes. A Nigerian on the minimum wage needs about 3.5 hours. Denmark has the priciest petrol in the EU, and Norway is also among the most expensive in Europe. Yet a litre costs only about 3.5 minutes of average pay in either country. (Section 4 gives the inputs.)

1. The video, checked

So is “3 to 6 cents” accurate? For over-quota fuel since 8 September, yes: 4.3 to 6.0 cents, depending on which exchange rate you use. Before 8 September, 3 cents was the old top price at the official rate. For fuel inside the quota, no: it costs 0.65 to 1.8 cents. According to the national refining and distribution company, the two quota tiers (110 litres a month) cover the needs of about 85% of drivers.1 So the litre a typical Iranian driver buys costs about a cent or less. At the open-market rate, a 50-litre tank at the first-tier price costs about 33 US cents. OUR MATH

A reporting disagreement we checked. AP (7 Sep)13 and Al Jazeera (8 Sep)14 described the change as “50,000 rials per litre for up to 110 litres,” doubling above that. Iran International, RFE/RL and MEES instead gave 15,000 and 30,000 rials for the two quota tiers.2,9,11 We checked Persian-language reporting of the official announcement. The deputy oil minister and the national refining and distribution company both say tiers 1 and 2 stayed at 1,500 and 3,000 toman (15,000 and 30,000 rials), and only the third tier went from 5,000 to 10,000 toman.1,8 (A toman is ten rials, the unit Iranians use in everyday speech.) Our reading: 50,000 rials was the old third-tier price, and the AP wording merged the tiers. We have not seen the government’s own written notice, only reporting of it.

Two exchange rates, and why every Iran dollar figure is a choice

Iran has an official exchange rate and a street rate, and in mid-September they were about 40% apart: roughly 1.65 million rials per dollar officially (16 Sep)4 and 2.3 million on the open market (17 Sep).3 Any dollar price for Iranian fuel depends on which one you pick, so we show both. The open-market rate is closer to what an ordinary Iranian can actually get.

One warning for readers who use aggregator sites. The dollar figure an aggregator shows for Iranian petrol may not match the rial price converted at the market rate it says it uses, so check the arithmetic before quoting it. We convert every rial price ourselves: at the Bonbast rate (17 Sep), 15,000 rials is about $0.0065. OUR MATH

Why the cheapest fuel on earth comes with a queue

  • Iran uses more gasoline than it makes. Estimates differ by period and by whether blending is counted. Consumption was roughly 129 to 145 million litres a day in mid-2026, against domestic output of roughly 121 to 122 million.15,5,13,16 Imports used to fill the gap. NEWS
  • The war cut both halves. Strikes since 28 February 2026 hit Iranian energy infrastructure, and reporting says refining capacity was damaged in the early weeks. We have not found a verifiable measure of how much. A US naval blockade reimposed on 14 July then choked imports.5,9 NEWS
  • The response was rationing before repricing. The second-tier quota was cut from 100 litres to 70 at the start of the Iranian year (late March) and then to 50.11 In August there were queues in Tehran and 20-litre caps per fill-up.5,17 The top tier was then doubled on 8 September. Parliament speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf said: “we need to save gasoline in our consumption.”18 NEWS
  • Price below cost invites smuggling. A litre that sells for well under a cent in Iran sells for a dollar or more across a border. That gap is the smuggler’s wage. Estimates of how much fuel leaves the country run from about 6 million litres a day (measured entering Pakistan, per traders quoted by RFE/RL, May 2026)19 to about 20 million (an Iranian official, May 2025)20 and up to 50 million (an Iranian economist, Oct 2024).21 Some of the “missing” fuel may be a quirk of how card entitlements are counted.16 Nobody measures it directly. NEWS

What it costs the state, and who gets the benefit

The IMF estimates that in 2024 Iran’s explicit fossil-fuel subsidies came to about $71bn, or 26% of GDP. That covers all fuels, including natural gas and electricity, not just petrol. Counting the unpriced costs of pollution and climate damage raises the total to about $180bn (66% of GDP).22 Shares of GDP for Iran depend heavily on which exchange rate is used to value its economy, so treat them as approximate. Separately, President Masoud Pezeshkian said Iran spent about $6bn importing gasoline in the year to 20 March 2026.15 NEWS

Quotas are issued per vehicle, so the benefit goes to car owners. A December 2025 reform limited subsidised fuel to one vehicle per household and excluded new, high-value and foreign cars.23,24 Households without a car get nothing directly. The government says revenue from the September increase will support people’s livelihoods.2,25 NEWS

Why any price change in Iran is political

On 15 November 2019, Iran raised petrol from 10,000 rials a litre to 15,000 for the first 60 litres a month and to 30,000 above that. Nationwide protests followed. Amnesty International documented at least 321 people killed (July 2022 update).26 Iranian officials gave lower figures: about 200 from the interior minister27 and 230 from a senior MP.28 That history helps explain why the 2025 and 2026 changes left the first-tier price untouched and loaded the increases onto the top tier instead.

Diesel in Iran: partly resolved

Persian financial press put subsidised diesel at 300 toman (3,000 rials) a litre and the unsubsidised price at 600 toman (6,000 rials).29 That is about 0.13 to 0.26 US cents at the open-market rate. OUR MATH The 1405 budget bill proposed pricing station-card diesel at 30% of the refinery purchase price, about 10,748 toman (107,480 rials).29 As of 12 September 2026, no new nationwide diesel increase had been announced.8 We could not confirm whether the budget proposal was ever implemented, or what the 2026 diesel quota rules are. Treat Iranian diesel figures as indicative.

3. Why a litre costs under a cent in one place and $2.96 in another

A pump price is a stack of layers: crude oil, refining, transport and retail margin, and tax. Subsidies take a layer away. Most of the gap between countries comes from the tax layer and the subsidy layer, not from the fuel itself.

The first lesson: before tax, fuel costs roughly the same across rich markets. About $1.00 in the US, $1.18 in the UK and $1.26 across the EU (all 14 Sep). The big differences at the pump are political choices about tax.

The second lesson: a fixed tax works like a shock absorber. Most fuel duty is a fixed number of pence or cents per litre, not a percentage. Picture a bucket that already holds a lot of water: pour in one more cup and the level rises only a little in percentage terms. Between February and September, the EU’s pre-tax petrol price rose 53% (23 Feb to 14 Sep). Tax per litre barely moved, from EUR 0.925 to EUR 0.976, with most of that increase coming from VAT.7 So the pump price rose only 26%. The UK shows the same pattern: pre-tax up 53%, pump up 28%. In the US, where tax is a thin slice, the pre-tax price rose 57% and the pump price 47%. OUR MATH The shock absorber has a limit: in euros and pounds the rise per litre was still large, about EUR 0.43 and 36p.

The third lesson: a cap does not make the cost disappear. Saudi Arabia’s petrol price has not moved; it has been capped since 2021.40 China’s price bureau overrode its own pricing formula from 23 March. In one step the formula called for a rise of 2,205 yuan a tonne, and the actual rise was 1,160.77 India froze prices for 76 days before four rises in May,78,45 and Mexico kept regular petrol near a voluntary 24-peso cap.79 In each case the war’s cost still lands on someone: a state budget, a state oil company’s accounts, or future borrowing. It shows up months later, in places a pump-price table cannot see.

What the table says. The ranking by dollar price and the ranking by affordability are nearly reversed. Denmark has some of the most expensive petrol in the EU, but on average pay a litre takes about 3.6 minutes. A Brazilian on the minimum wage works 53 minutes for one. A Nigerian on the minimum wage works about three and a half hours, and a full 50-litre tank costs about a month’s minimum wage. Nigeria removed its petrol subsidy, so its pump price now follows world markets, while its wages do not. Iran is the mirror image: a tier-1 litre costs about a minute of minimum-wage work. At the import price, the same litre would cost about an hour, which puts it in Brazilian and Egyptian territory. The cheap litre depends on the subsidy.

Who a fuel subsidy actually helps

A fuel subsidy is a discount handed out per litre, so the people who burn the most litres get the most discount. Those tend to be the people with the most cars and the biggest ones. The IMF’s December 2025 update finds that, across countries, for every dollar of explicit fossil-fuel subsidy the poorest 20% of households receive about 8 cents and the richest 20% receive 41 cents.22 For petrol specifically, an earlier IMF study (Coady, Flamini and Sears, 2015) estimated that it takes about $33 of subsidy to deliver $1 to the poorest fifth.117 The same 2025 IMF paper adds an important caveat: removing subsidies without compensation also hurts the poor, so how the savings are recycled matters. Wartime tax cuts share the same feature. They help most the people who drive most. We have not found a study measuring that for the 2026 cuts.

5. What the war changed

  • The crude price. Dated Brent went from $71 a barrel (27 Feb) to a $138 peak (7 Apr) and $131 (15 Sep).72,73
  • Where governments let prices move, drivers felt it: the US (+47% petrol, to 14 Sep), Canada (+34%, to 15 Sep), South Africa (+34%, from 2 Sep), Finland (+35%, to 14 Sep), and unsubsidised petrol in Malaysia (+55%, week from 10 Sep). (Sources as in the Section 2 table.)
  • Where governments held prices, budgets felt it instead: Saudi Arabia (0%), Iran’s quota tiers (0%), Mexico (+0.5%), India (+8%), Japan (+8% after subsidy) and China (+17% under a cap).
  • Where tax is heavy, the percentage shock was smaller: the EU (+26%) and the UK (+28%), against crude up 83%.
  • Diesel took the bigger hit nearly everywhere prices were free to move.
  • Iran is where cheap fuel met physical scarcity. Rationing came first, and the price rise followed only for the heaviest users.

6. What we do not know

  • Iran’s exchange rate. There is more than one official rate as well as an open-market rate. Every dollar figure for Iran is a choice, which is why we show two.
  • Iran’s official notice. The tier structure is confirmed through several independent reports of the deputy oil minister’s and the distribution company’s statements. We have not read the government’s written notice itself.
  • Iranian diesel. Current prices are reported in the Persian press; 2026 quota rules and the status of the budget proposal are unconfirmed.
  • Iran’s supply numbers. Consumption estimates range from 129 to 145 million litres a day and production estimates from 107 to 122 million, depending on the period and on whether blending is counted.23,16,13 Refinery war damage has been reported but not quantified by any source we can verify.
  • Smuggling. Estimates run from 6 to 50 million litres a day, and nobody measures it directly.
  • Hong Kong’s paid price. Discounts depend on the card and the day; there is no official average transaction price.
  • Libya and Venezuela. We found no official price list from either national oil company. Libya’s price comes from an IMF report and the press (latest found: May 2025). Venezuela’s multi-tier dollar pricing makes “the price” ambiguous.
  • Lags. Norway’s latest official figure is July, taken during a tax holiday that ended 1 September. Nigeria’s statistics office last published May 2026 (petrol NGN 1,596, May average),118 so we used September street prices. Wage data are 6–18 months old.
  • The hidden bill. Where prices were capped, the cost will surface in budgets and state oil company accounts months from now.

If any figure here turns out to be wrong, we will correct it on this page, with the date, the old figure struck through, the new one and its source.
