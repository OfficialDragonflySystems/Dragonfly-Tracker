Research method · Our own failures

Is Your Backtest Lying To You?

Nine ways ours did, nine checks that would have caught most of them, what those checks still do not catch, and how the ideas did once the tests were fixed.

Published 13 November 2026. Most figures were re-checked against our own records on 6 and 7 November 2026, and the broker fee pages were re-read on 7 November; each figure below carries the date it refers to. All dollar amounts are US dollars from simulated tests, not money we made or lost.

We run many research systems, and a few of them have spent months building trading strategies. Almost every strong early result we found fell apart when we checked it, and the cause was usually in the data, the arithmetic or the counting. Here is what happened, so you can check your own work for the same things. A broken test does not prove an idea is dead, so after each fix we tested again. A section near the end says how those retests came out, and most of them failed too.

The nine checks
  1. Too good: suspect the data first, and scan for impossible moves and repeated timestamps.
  2. Units: match every multiplier to the exchange, then push one hand-calculated trade through the code.
  3. Stuck tracker: a forward test must read the feed it claims to read, and every open item needs an age.
  4. Luck: count every variation you tried, and recompute the bar when the count changes.
  5. Roll: when a result disagrees sharply with research, audit your accounting before believing either.
  6. One window: split the history into equal windows and look at each.
  7. One market: run the same fixed rule on other markets.
  8. Costs: use the real fee for each instrument, dated, and test the effect against the friction.
  9. Frozen price: check every price's age and contract month, and record the real bid and ask.

1. The number was too good

On 1 September 2026 one test returned a 96 percent win rate and a profit of about 4.1 million dollars on a single S&P micro futures contract: 5,552 trades of a mean-reversion rule, from May 2019 to that date. A result that extreme is a reason to stop and reconcile the prices, the trades and the profit before believing it. (How this got past every alarm we had is the subject of an earlier piece, Our Data Was Lying for Five Months. Here it is the first of nine checks, with a cause we found only after that piece was published.)

When we did, our price history for that contract turned out to hold two versions of the same price, mixed together under one name: our data vendor's back-adjusted series and the unadjusted front-month prices from our own collector. The two sit at different levels, so the mixed series jumped by several percent for no reason. Counting adjacent 60-minute bars on 7 November, 29 percent of the mixed series' bars to 26 May 2026 moved more than 2 percent from the bar before (12,678 of 43,598), against 0.14 percent in the vendor's own file (57 of 41,305). Our count on 1 September, with the table's several labels for this timeframe combined, was 31.8 percent; on the single label we test now, it is about 29 to 30 percent, depending on which duplicate rows are kept (see below). A series that oscillates for no reason tends to flatter a mean-reversion strategy, because every dip looks like a bounce.

On the vendor's series alone, which ends on 26 May 2026, the same rule made 1,683 trades, won 28 percent of them and lost about 27,500 dollars. Both runs charged the same costs: 2.50 dollars a side plus two ticks of slippage. We reran both on 7 November. The clean result reproduced to the dollar. The mixed series did not reproduce its first number, and neither the 5,552-trade run nor a rerun later the same day (4,731 trades, 94 percent, about 3.3 million dollars) can be regenerated. Run now on the same table with the same code, it gives 4,763 trades, a 93.5 percent win rate and about 3.3 million dollars on the full file, and 4,660 trades, a 95 percent win rate and about 3.3 million dollars to 26 May 2026. Those two differ only because they cover different windows, since our collector keeps adding rows. So we quote the 4.1 million as what we saw on 1 September and not as a number anyone can regenerate. Every version is impossible, which was the point.

We now know why they cannot be regenerated. The table of 60-minute S&P micro bars held two rows for the same hour in 35,959 of its 43,599 hours to 26 May 2026, and in every one of those the two closing prices differ, by a median of 232 index points. The rows look as if two different processes wrote them: 42,231 carry a timestamp in one format and 37,327 in another. Our loaders then removed the “duplicates” with a sort that does not say which of the two rows survives, so the series we tested was a different mixture of the two price levels every time the rows were read in a different order. We checked by changing only that choice, with the same strategy code and window (research/corrupt_vs_clean_mes.py, run on a copy). Keeping the row saved first for each hour gives 1,699 trades, a 33 percent win rate and 0.39 percent of bars with a jump over 2 percent, close to, though not the same as, the vendor's file. Keeping the row saved last gives 1,805 trades and an 86 percent win rate. Our script's own clean-up gave 4,726 trades, a 94.8 percent win rate and jumps in 30 percent of bars. The oscillation that looked like a mean-reversion edge was created when the data was loaded, by which duplicate happened to survive.

Check 1. If a result is too good to be true, suspect the data first, then make it concrete: scan for single-bar moves of many standard deviations and for repeated timestamps (35,959 repeated hours here). When you remove duplicates, choose the row to keep by a stated rule, never by sort order, and show that the result does not change with the choice. Check flagged bars against a second source before you change anything, and look for missing intervals, impossible highs and lows and daylight-saving jumps. A warning, not a proof: a subtle data error can produce a believable result, so this catches the loud errors only.

2. The units were wrong

Until 7 September our code valued a Micro Bitcoin contract with the full-size contract's dollars-per-point, which made it 50 times too large (CME defines the micro as one tenth of a bitcoin and the standard contract as five). The cost charged per trade was a fixed dollar amount (a flat 2.50 dollars a side in the tester until 7 September, then 85 cents for micro contracts), so it looked tiny next to the inflated profits. On the last ranking saved before we removed those results, the top six places were all Micro Bitcoin.

That ranking was invalid, because the multiplier inflated profit against a fixed cost. It does not by itself prove the rules had no edge, so we checked: once the multiplier was fixed and the inflated entries were purged on 10 September, the 23 forward trades in item 3 lost about 2,500 dollars.

Check 2. For every instrument, compare tick size, tick value and contract multiplier to the exchange's published specification, then push one hand-calculated trade through the real code and see that the dollars match. One trade is a floor, not a test: also try a short, a multi-contract trade, a partial exit and both fee directions, and keep each specification with the date you read it.

3. The tracker was stuck

Our forward test, the one that was meant to be a clean look at new data, turned out on 7 October to be reading a file that stopped on 26 May. Of its 56 open trades, 29 had entry bars inside the frozen stretch and could never close, because the file never grew, and the scoreboard showed them as healthy pending evidence. Those two counts come from our 7 October audit note: the ledger backup from that day holds 97 rows (63 open, 29 resolved, 5 marked unresolvable) and does not reproduce the 56 and 29.

The same review found the multiplier from item 2 still on that scoreboard: 23 Micro Bitcoin trades, across ten rule variants, had their dollars stored before the fix and showed a combined loss of about 114,000 dollars. Recomputed with the right multiplier, the loss is between about 2,400 and 2,500 dollars, depending on the per-side fee used (2.50 gave 2,438, our later 3.40 gave 2,479, and the 2.01 we read on the broker's pages on 7 November would give about 2,416; see item 8).

Check 3. A forward test must read the feed it claims to read, and every open item needs an age. If an item goes stale it is flagged and shown separately as unresolved. It is never quietly deleted. Stored results need recomputing when the inputs behind them change, and the original ledger should be kept with a dated correction beside it. Watch the feed's own last timestamp as well as each position's age: a slow position can be old while its data is fresh.

4. The luck correction was out of date

When you try thousands of variations, the best one looks good by luck alone, so you raise the bar it has to clear. Our bar is the expected best Sharpe ratio of that many random strategies (equation 1 in Bailey and López de Prado, "The Deflated Sharpe Ratio", 2014), using the mean and spread of random results measured on our own data. That is only the benchmark half of their method. Their full test, the deflated Sharpe ratio, is the same idea with the threshold raised for the number of trials, and it also adjusts for the length of the record and for skew and fat tails; ours does not do that, so clearing our bar is a first filter and not a significance test.

Until 7 October our bar assumed 26,781 attempts, all pooled together, a number left over from a wider list of markets. The search grid at that point was 10,428 across nine markets: 1,422 variations for each of seven markets and 237 for each of the two crypto contracts. The fall is partly because we had removed markets whose data or multiplier we found to be wrong, so 10,428 is the size of the current grid and not a count of everything we have tried, and a smaller count lowers the bar in our favour. The pooled results behind the old bar of 1.18 were overwritten, so we cannot show the exact change. Holding the spread of random results at 0.29, which would reproduce that shift (today's board shows about 0.23), the formula gives about 1.19 for 26,781 attempts and about 1.12 for 10,428. That is a reconstruction, not a saved result.

The bigger change came from comparing each market against its own luck baseline instead of one pooled baseline. In the run of 1 November the bar is about 0.57 for platinum, 0.59 for Nasdaq micro futures and 1.62 for gold, so the split did not simply lower the bar. Each of those bars counts only that market's own variations (1,422, or 237 for the crypto contracts), not the 10,428 across all nine, which is lenient: a bar that also paid for picking the best market would be higher. If every market's bar used all 10,428 attempts, the same run would give about 0.69 for platinum, 0.70 for Nasdaq micro and 1.94 for gold. The bars also move, and more than that sentence suggests. Two weeks earlier gold's was 1.27. Read from the raw board file on 7 November (158 entries, saved after 1 November), the platinum bar is 0.27, not 0.57, and the Nasdaq micro bar 1.44, not 0.59, so one went down by half and the other more than doubled within a week. Gold's random results come from only five entries on the 1 November board (seven now), the smallest sample we allow, and our S&P micro bar rests on a pooled fallback because that market has too few entries of its own. A single bar should be read as indicative.

Check 4. Count every variation you tried, including the ones you dropped, and recompute the bar whenever that count changes. Variations that are near copies of each other count for less than independent ones, so the raw count overstates the independent tries (the paper gives a way to estimate the independent number, which we have not applied), while research outside the grid is not in it at all. The honest report shows the bar before and after the change so readers can see which way it moved.

5. We treated a contract change as a return

A well-documented effect, carry (Koijen, Moskowitz, Pedersen and Vrugt, 'Carry', published in the Journal of Financial Economics in 2018), came out at a Sharpe ratio of minus 1.4 on its first run, on 21 September. That paper itself reports carry doing badly in some periods, so a bad number alone proves nothing. It was the size of the gap from the research that sent us back to check the accounting. We found that in our futures universe only 4.4 percent of consecutive month-ends sat on the same contract. In the other 95.6 percent we were comparing the price of two different contracts, the expiring one and the next one, so the measured monthly 'return' was the gap between contracts and not the change in anything we could have held. It looked like an inverted sign because that gap is correlated with the carry signal by construction.

Check 5. When a result disagrees sharply with published research, audit your accounting before you believe either one. Do not force the sign to match the paper either, because that is another way of fitting. Do the same audit on results that agree with the paper: agreement can hide an offsetting error.

6. The result lived in one stretch of history

After we fixed the roll error, carry showed a Sharpe ratio of 0.63 before costs and 0.41 after 10 basis points of cost, over 214 months from March 2008 to December 2025. That looked fine. Then we cut it into six three-year windows (the first has 34 months, the rest 36). 2011 to 2013 came in at 1.44 and produced about half of the return: 48 percent of the summed monthly returns, 45 percent of the compounded growth, and 53 percent on a log-wealth basis, which is the one that adds up across windows (the six compounded shares sum to about 80 percent, not 100, because compounding is not additive). Every window from 2014 on sat between 0.12 and 0.25. Those are small positive numbers, not zero, but they are below what we would trade.

Check 6. Split the history into equal windows (six three-year windows here; halves and thirds work too) and look at every one, with the split chosen before you look. If one slice carries most of the profit, the idea rests on a rare episode and not on a steady edge, which changes how long you might wait for it to pay. Do not simply delete the best slice and call the rest the honest estimate: that is another choice made after seeing the result. Real effects can lose for years, so a sign flip is a reason to mark an idea down, not a rule to throw it out.

7. One market trended and everything looked good

Platinum futures fell 43 percent from January 2008 to March 2017, then rose 64 percent to May 2026, the two halves of our test (changes between the end points of our continuous, ratio-adjusted contract, not what someone holding and rolling futures would have earned). A rule that only works in one market's long trends tells you little about the rule. On 7 October, 18 families of rules cleared our bar according to our notes, 16 of them on platinum; the promotion list we froze the same day has 19 entries, 16 on platinum and 3 on Nasdaq micro, and we have not reconciled the extra Nasdaq entry.

On 21 September we had already tested the idea with a fixed grid of eight settings of one trend rule (four breakout lengths times two band widths), written down before the run, on platinum and, as controls, on copper and silver. The score was each market's average profit factor across the eight settings, before costs: total winnings divided by total losses, with the bar set by what random strategies with the same number of trades would reach. Platinum scored 1.06 against a bar of 1.34, silver 1.20 against 1.43, and copper 0.78, which means the rule lost money there. With costs, platinum fell to 1.02.

Check 7. Test the same fixed rule on other markets, and discount any pass that leans on a single one. Markets that move together can share the same lucky stretch, so look at what each market adds. Say beforehand which markets the idea should work in, and treat markets you have already looked at as used up.

8. Costs

We charged commission, but not always the right commission. Until 7 September our shadow tester charged a flat 2.50 dollars a side on every contract. From 7 September to 7 October it charged 85 cents a side on every micro contract, Micro Bitcoin included: the all-in rate we had assumed for stock-index micros, applied to a crypto contract that is priced differently. On 7 October we changed Micro Bitcoin to 3.40 dollars a side (a 2.25 commission plus 1.15 of exchange and regulatory fees, as we read the broker's and CME's fee pages that day) and Micro Ether to 30 cents.

When we re-read those pages on 7 November, they did not match what we had used. The broker's US and Canadian futures-commission pages both list the Micro Bitcoin commission at 85 cents a contract at the lowest volume tier (up to 1,000 contracts a month). The 2.25 figure sits on those pages only against other micro crypto products. The exchange-fee page lists 1.15 for Micro Bitcoin and a separate one-cent regulatory charge, so today's total is about 2.01 dollars a side. We cannot recover what the pages said on 7 October, so we do not know whether 3.40 was ever right, and our account is with the broker's Canadian entity (the Canadian and US pages show the same rates). Either way 85 cents was too low for this contract, by a factor of about 2.4 at 2.01 and 4 at 3.40. On 7 November we set Micro Bitcoin in our cost register to the 2.01 the pages now show; the shadow rows recorded between 7 October and then still carry 3.40. Micro Ether comes to 31 cents on the same pages (20, 10 and 1), close to the 30 we used. And the stock-index micros that the 85 cents came from list 25 cents plus 35 plus 1, which is 61 cents: the rate was about 40 percent too high for the family it was assumed from.

Separately, we tested a published effect (Gao, Han, Li and Zhou, 'Market intraday momentum', Journal of Financial Economics, 2018), in which earlier moves in the day predict the last half-hour, on four full-size futures (S&P 500, Nasdaq, Russell 2000 and gold). Our version is an adaptation. Of the four signal windows we tried, the closest to the paper's is the return from the previous close to 10 am, predicting the final half-hour; the others change the start or the end. From 2008 to 2018 the timing effect was strong before costs, with a studentized z of about 5 against a permutation null, above the corrected bar of about 3.15 across the 66 variants we tried. But it was worth only about 1 to 5 basis points of the contract's value per trade for the index futures (under 1 for gold), and costs were 3 to 6. From 2019 on, most versions were near zero before costs.

Check 8. Look up the real fee for each instrument separately (a rate that suits one family of contracts is wrong for another), date the schedule you used, charge it from the first run, and test whether the effect is bigger than the friction. Commission is only one component of cost; the spread, your size and your fills matter as well.

9. One price had frozen

We found this one on 14 October, and it is the cleanest example we have. A pairs test showed micro gold and full-size gold drifting apart, then snapping back, and the model made money. In its first week, 23 to 29 September, it showed a gain of about 23,000 dollars across 99 trades, after our assumed commission. From 30 September to 14 October it stopped working: 37 trades netted about minus 1,700 dollars.

We do not know why the first week worked. The prices in that week were moving, not frozen, and at exit the two legs were typically within about 0.01 percent of each other and never more than 0.2 percent apart. That shows the two prices were close, not that both legs were the same contract month, and we have not checked the contract months for that week. Our best guess is that last-trade prices bouncing between the bid and the ask made the spread look like it was reverting. We cannot test that for September, because we were not yet recording real quotes.

We found a defect in the losing period. 30 September was first notice day for the October gold contract, the first day on which delivery notices can be issued (the last business day of the month before the delivery month, under COMEX's rule as quoted to us; we could not open the rulebook ourselves). We were still watching that contract: we had been rolling about five days before the last trading day, not before first notice day. When we recorded real quotes next to the test's prices on 13 and 14 October, the micro gold price in the test was identical for all 1,321 readings, 4209.10, while the live quote moved from about 4161 to 4090.

We now roll when the contract's last trading day, which our code approximates as the 27th of the delivery month, is less than 30 days away. Worked out from the exchange's rule, that lands between 0 and 3 business days before first notice day: for the December 2026 contract we roll on Friday 27 November against first notice on Monday 30 November, and for June 2027 on the same day. That margin is thin, and calendar-day arithmetic can fall on a weekend. The sturdier rule is to key the roll to first notice day minus a set number of business days, which ours is not yet. (These dates are computed from the rule, not read from CME's calendar.)

The recorder then answered the bigger question. From 13 October to 5 November it captured real quotes on 118 round trips across seven pairs of contracts. Our model's profit figure is net of an assumed commission (14,500 dollars over these trips) and one tick of slippage on every fill (20,800 dollars); on that basis the trips lost about 41,500. Before any cost they lost about 6,100. We then measured the real bid and ask at each fill: against the model's prices they cost 35,600. Taking the real cost in place of the assumed slippage, and keeping commission, the 118 trips lost about 56,200 dollars. Leaving out the frozen-gold days does not rescue it: the 114 trips from 15 October gained about 4,400 before cost and lost about 52,700 after commission and real quotes. On 12 October, before any quotes existed and after the model had already lost money on its own prices for two weeks, we had written down that a result of zero or less after 100 valid round trips would be taken as showing the modelled profit did not survive real prices, and that the system would come off our candidate list. It has met that condition. That shows the system fails at real quotes; it does not by itself show why the first week made money.

Check 9. For every price in a test, check how old it is and which contract month it is, using the source's own timestamp and not the time you fetched it. A price that does not change for much longer than that market normally goes between trades is a flag, not proof. Record the real bid and ask next to the price you assumed, and never count the same friction twice: if the model already charges a slippage allowance, do not also subtract the measured spread on top.

What this adds up to

Basic checks are cheap, and skipping them is expensive. Reliable contract histories, full experiment logs and real depth-of-book data are not cheap. Some of the checks now run in code. A leg whose latest price is more than fifteen minutes old stops producing signals (fifteen minutes is our own threshold for these liquid contracts, a coarse alarm and not a standard, and far too loose to guarantee two prices are in step to a tick). Contract specs sit in one dated register copied from the exchange's pages, which tests itself for internal consistency and flags any other definition in our code that disagrees, though it cannot notice an exchange changing a spec. Since 8 October our ranking board will not add a result that has not passed the era split and the cross-market test. The 19 it added on 7 October, a day before that rule, are still listed and have not yet been scored on new data. The cost check and the fee lookup are still manual.

How the ideas did once the tests were fixed

Clean data did not rescue most of them, and one passed our test for existing but not our test for being worth holding. A clean test is not a promise of a good answer. It is how you find out you do not have one before it costs you money.

What the nine checks do not catch

They do not catch a rule that quietly uses information it could not have had at the time. A real audit for that is concrete: for every input to every signal, record when it became available and confirm the decision used only what existed before it; fit any rolling estimate on past data only; do not act on a bar's close before the bar has finished; and do not choose a contract using information from later in the same day. The nine checks above do not include it.

They do not tell you your promotion rule has been tuned to the history it was built on. Choosing the era-split and cross-market hurdles after seeing failures is itself research, and it has to be judged on data it has not seen. And a record of quotes is still a simulation, not proof you would have been filled at those prices.

Every return needs a denominator. A dollar profit on one contract says little about the capital behind it, and Sharpe ratios computed on monthly, daily or per-trade returns are not comparable until each is labelled. Ours are stated beside each figure, and none of them is a statement about uncertainty: a count of signals is not a count of independent experiments.

A note for anyone using AI to build or test strategies: an AI can produce a convincing backtest in minutes. It does nothing to make one true, and none of these checks get cheaper.

If you want a second pair of eyes on a backtest of your own, say so. We would rather tell you it is lying before you put money behind it.

Our call

Frozen when this is published, scored in public. The three micro-versus-full-size index pairs (micro and E-mini S&P 500, micro and E-mini Nasdaq-100, micro and E-mini Russell 2000) are built to settle to the same index value at final settlement (CME says so for its Micro E-mini contracts), so we think most of the modelled profit is price noise and spread that disappears at real quotes. We do not claim the gap between them stays within any set size before settlement; nothing we have read says it does.

The call: by 31 March 2027 (4:00 pm Eastern), using our quote-based shadow records for the three pairs of micro and E-mini index futures that we have been recording (MES with ES, MNQ with NQ, and M2K with RTY, on the same contract month in each leg and in the ten-to-one ratio of micro to E-mini), at least one pair with 30 or more completed round trips will show a profit on model prices that is greater than zero and, for the same trades at the same size, keep at least half of that profit when each fill is instead charged at the real bid (for sells) and real ask (for buys) recorded at that moment. Both measures charge the same commission, 0.85 dollars per micro contract and 2.50 dollars per E-mini contract per side, and neither includes any assumed slippage. Only round trips entered at or after 9:00 am Eastern on 13 November 2026 count; a round trip is complete when both legs are closed. Positions open at the deadline do not count toward the 30 but are valued at the quote that would close them for the profit test. Every qualifying round trip counts; none is dropped for being a loser. A quote older than 5 seconds, or a missing bid or ask on either leg, makes that round trip not qualify and it is listed. The result is evaluated once, after the deadline, on all qualifying round trips. If no pair has 30 qualifying round trips, the call is unresolved. We will publish the result either way.

Why the wording is this exact. Our first wording, approved on 26 October, left five things open that reviewers found before publication: what “model prices” meant (our recorded figure already took off an assumed tick of slippage, so charging the real spread on top would have counted slippage twice), when the counting starts, what counts as a profit, which contracts, and what happens to stale quotes. The version above closes each of them. The commission figures are the fixed settings our shadow has used since it started; the broker’s published rate for index micros is lower (see item 8), and using one figure in both measures keeps the comparison fair. A 5-second limit on quote age would have excluded about 2 percent of the quote records so far.

Our probability: about one in four. This is our judgment, not a figure calibrated from the records below: it allows for one pair having a real relationship wider than the spread we pay. A pass would show that a shadow strategy survived recorded quotes, not that those fills were available to us.

Where it starts. These records do not count toward the call, but you should see them. To 5 November the three pairs had 77 round trips. Typical sizes were 40 micro S&P contracts against 4 E-mini (10 to 1), 20 micro Nasdaq against 2 E-mini, and 100 micro Russell against 10 E-mini, and the average half of the bid-ask spread on the recorded fills ran from about 0.6 to 2.5 ticks a leg. Before any cost, the three pairs made about 13,200 dollars over the 77 trips; our assumed commission took 10,500 and the real bid-ask cost against model prices was 28,500, leaving about minus 25,900.
