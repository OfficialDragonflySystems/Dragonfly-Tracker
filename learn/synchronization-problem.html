The Synchronization Problem

The AI build-out is quoted in announcements — “a 10-gigawatt campus,” “$750 billion of capex.” But almost none of that is compute yet. Every input a data center needs arrives on its own clock, and the slowest one sets the real date. The gap between announced and deployable is the whole story — and most coverage misses it. Hover or tap any underlined term.

Dragonfly Lens · August 1, 2026 · A framework, not a forecast.

The short version

  • Announced is not deployed. Roughly 12 GW of new US data-center capacity is slated to come online in 2026 — but only about one-third is actually under construction, and by some counts half of planned builds have slipped or been cancelled.
  • The loudest input is the fastest. GPUs ship in weeks and get all the headlines. The real gate is the quiet stuff: a grid connection can take five to seven years; a large power transformer, two to four.
  • The slowest input sets the date. Like a dinner party where every dish has to hit the table at once, a data center only runs when the last piece arrives. That’s the synchronization tax.
  • The takeaway is a tool. We give you a deployment-gate scorecard — a short checklist that turns any “5 GW announced!” headline into a realistic date, and separates the firms that compress the critical path from the ones just riding it.

The number that lies

When a hyperscaler announces a “5-gigawatt AI campus,” the number does a magic trick: it turns a construction project with a decade of dependencies into something that sounds like it’s already humming. It isn’t. A gigawatt announced is a gigawatt of intent — permits to file, equipment to order, a grid connection to wait in line for. The compute that actually earns revenue is years behind the press release.

The 2026 numbers make the gap concrete. Hyperscalers have guided to more than $750 billion in data-center capital spending this year. Yet of the roughly 12 GW of new US capacity planned to energize in 2026, only about a third is under active construction — and industry trackers estimate that 30–50% of announced builds are delayed or cancelled outright, choked by shortages of power equipment and parts. The announcements are running years ahead of the concrete.

Why this matters for your money: a stock, a sector, or a whole thesis can be priced as if “announced” equals “online next quarter.” The distance between the two is where both the disappointment (capex booked years before revenue) and the opportunity (whoever shortens that distance) actually live.

The dinner-party problem

Picture cooking dinner for 500 people, where every dish has to reach the table at the same minute. Your oven, your delivery truck, and your prep cook each run on a different schedule. It doesn’t matter how fast the salad is ready — if the main course is two hours out, nobody eats until then. The latest item sets dinner.

A data center is that dinner party. A world-class chef — the newest, fastest GPUs — standing in a kitchen with no power hookup serves exactly nothing. Engineers call the longest chain of must-happen-in-order steps the critical path. In the AI build-out, the loudest, most-tracked input — the chips — is almost never on it. The critical path runs through the quiet, unglamorous, long-lead items nobody live-tweets.

Read the bottom of that table slowly. A large transformer now averages roughly 128 weeks — about two and a half years — and high-capacity units can run four to five years, versus two before 2020. The interconnection queue holds more than 2,000 GW of projects nationwide, with typical waits near five years and the busiest grid regions pushing seven. And every AI accelerator needs its HBM, whose entire 2026 output was sold out — spoken for by the biggest buyers — before the year even began.

So the chip you read about is real, but it’s the salad. The dinner is set by a transformer being wound in a factory backlog and a utility study that started years ago.

The synchronization tax

Here’s the cruel arithmetic: because the inputs must all arrive together, a delay in any one of them delays the whole site. Speed up the GPUs and you’ve sped up nothing — they weren’t the gate. Slip the transformer by six months and the entire campus slips six months, GPUs sitting in a warehouse the whole time, capital already spent.

That is the synchronization tax, and it’s why the money and the reality fall out of step. The capital is committed up front — land, chips, contracts — but the revenue can’t start until the last, slowest piece lands. Spending happens on the announcement’s clock; earnings happen on the interconnection queue’s clock. Those two clocks are years apart.

If that pattern — heavy capital laid down long before the cash flow shows up — sounds familiar, it should. It’s the exact mechanism behind the “is this a bubble?” debate we covered in the Austrian lens on the build-out. Synchronization risk is how a boom can build real, useful things and still strand the capital that built them: the demand may be genuine, but if the compute lands three years late into a changed world, the investment can still fail to earn its keep.

Who compresses the critical path

Once you see the build-out as a critical path, the investable question stops being “who sells the bottleneck?” and becomes sharper: who shortens the longest pole? The value doesn’t just accrue to whoever makes the scarce part — it accrues to whoever makes the date arrive sooner. A few ways that happens:

  • Behind-the-meter power — building generation at the site so you don’t wait five years in the interconnection queue. This directly attacks the longest pole, which is why energy-rich regions and “bring-your-own-power” models are suddenly central.
  • Long-lead pre-ordering — the operators quietly reserving transformers, switchgear, and HBM years ahead. Boring, and decisive: a secured slot in a two-year backlog is a moat.
  • Modular / prefabricated builds — factory-made data-center blocks that collapse construction time and run in parallel with the power work instead of after it.
  • Turnkey delivery — firms that own the whole messy sequence (power + shell + cooling + fit-out) and hand over a finished, energized facility, absorbing the synchronization risk themselves.

That reframing is the difference between “power is the hot theme” and knowing which exposure actually gets paid. A company that sells a scarce component into a project that’s still four years from energizing has a great story and a long wait. A company that shortens the wait has pricing power now.

The deployment-gate scorecard

Here’s the part you can use on the next headline you see. When a project is announced, don’t ask “how many gigawatts?” Ask which inputs are locked, which are pending, and which are unknown. The binding constraint — the true earliest date — is the longest-lead item that isn’t locked yet.

Run any “X GW announced” headline through this:

  1. Power secured? Is there a signed grid interconnection or on-site generation — or just a plan? (This is usually the gate. No power date = no compute date.)
  2. Transformers & switchgear ordered? Reserved slots, or still to be sourced into a multi-year backlog?
  3. Shell under construction? Steel in the ground, or a rendering?
  4. Chips + HBM allocated? A real allocation from a sold-out supply, or an intention to buy?
  5. Cooling + crews lined up? Liquid-cooling gear and skilled labor committed?
  6. Permits / water / community clear? Any pending approval that can stall or halt it?

Count the pending and unknown rows, find the one with the longest lead time, and that — not the gigawatt number — is roughly when the site becomes real. Most splashy announcements have their power and transformers in the “pending” column. Now you know what the headline left out.

This is the habit we’re after: not memorizing tickers, but owning a lens you can point at any new announcement and immediately see what’s missing. A headline gives you a number. The scorecard gives you a date — and the gap between them is the edge.

What to watch next

Because the real gates are quiet, the useful signals are quiet too. These are the gauges that tell you whether the announced-vs-deployable gap is closing or widening:

  • Transformer and switchgear order books & lead times — the single clearest read on the physical bottleneck. Lengthening = the gap is widening.
  • Interconnection-queue data — queue length and average wait by grid region (PJM, ERCOT, and the rest). Reform that shortens waits is a genuine catalyst.
  • HBM allocation commentary — when memory makers stop saying “sold out,” a chip constraint is easing.
  • Behind-the-meter project count — rising = operators routing around the grid, which is the market solving synchronization in real time.
  • Utilization vs. nameplate — how much announced capacity is actually energized and busy. The wider that gap, the more the announcements are outrunning reality.

The honest close

This lens cuts both ways, and that’s the point — it’s a framework, not a cheerleader. Synchronization risk is exactly why some of this capex will strand: build three years late into a world that changed, and even real demand may not save the return. It’s also why the firms that genuinely compress the critical path — who make the date arrive sooner — can capture outsized, durable value while everyone else waits in line.

What it is not is a reason to take any “5 GW announced” headline at face value. Nobody ships a data center. They assemble one, slowly, from a dozen supply chains that have to arrive together — and the slowest one, the one nobody’s tweeting about, decides when the lights actually turn on.
