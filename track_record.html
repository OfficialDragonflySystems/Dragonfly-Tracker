How we keep score

What we withdrew on 6 November 2026 and why, how every call is scored from now on, and what we are testing next.

What we withdrew, and why

We withdrew our historical table of 87 stocks.

In May 2026 we built a table of 87 stocks with entry dates going back to 2013. We never traded it. It was meant to be a historical test of our selection criteria with no look-ahead: each entry was to rest only on information that was public on the entry date, so we could learn what the method would have picked and carry that forward to new companies. We showed the result first as our track record and later as a backtest.

We re-test everything we publish, and this table has now been reviewed three times: in June 2026 (prices and arithmetic), in September 2026 (returns restated as annualised figures with dates), and on 6 November 2026, when we finally tested the entry dates themselves. That third review found the table did not meet its own standard. The dates had been chosen by hand with knowledge of how each stock went on to perform: 42 of the 87 entries fall on the first trading day of a year, 44 cite at least one event that happened after the entry date, and 7 could not have been bought as stated. A table built that way cannot show what the method would have found at the time, so we have withdrawn every return from it, original and corrected.

The review did not test whether the companies or the ideas behind them were good; that question is still open. What it did settle is how we test from now on: a selection rule is written down and dated before it is run, every input is dated before the entry it supports, and the rule is run across the whole universe so the stocks it would also have picked are counted too. The sections below are that standard in practice.

The full detail is in our corrections log, entry dated 6 November 2026.

How the record works from now on

Dated before the outcome, scored in public, losers kept.

  • Dated first. A call counts only if we published it, with its date, before the outcome was known. Nothing is added afterwards.
  • Checkable. Each call is one sentence a stranger could check, with our stated confidence, the date it will be decided by, and the source we will check it against.
  • Scored in public. When a call resolves we mark it hit or miss on the record and leave it there. The misses stay.

Today that record holds six dated forward calls: three resolved (3 hits, 0 misses), three still open. The first was dated 8 October 2026. That is too few to show anything yet, and we will not round it up. You can see it, and every call after it, in the Decision Ledger. The same calls are published as plain data for anyone, or any AI agent, who wants to check or quote them.

Signals we have published since 17 May 2026 are also signed when they go out, so they cannot be edited later without it showing. Check a signed signal yourself.

What we are testing next

A better way of deciding, tested before it is trusted.

Replay pre-registered 7 Nov 2026 · not yet run · no result exists

We should be plain about where we stand. We have tested a long list of ways to pick and time positions: intraday entry patterns in index futures, exit and stop rules, regime and sentiment filters, a scheduled overnight hold, and a hand-built conviction score. Nearly all of it failed to beat random choice once the data was clean, and the one result that looked strong turned out to rest on the table above. That is expensive knowledge, but it is knowledge: we know, with numbers, what does not work. What we do not yet have is a way of judging a company, a technology or an economy that is demonstrably clearer than chance.

Building that is the job now, and it has three parts. First, a pre-registered replay: the rules for scoring a company, the universe it is scored against (about 1,500 US stocks, including those that later failed or were delisted), the comparison (the S&P 500, the sector, and random picks on the same dates) and the pass-or-fail thresholds were written down and frozen on 7 November 2026, before any result exists. The parts of our score that were written with hindsight are excluded from the historical test. Second, a forward run: from the first month-end after the freeze, the same rules score every company in the universe each month and the scores are stored before the outcome, so in time we can show whether high scores were followed by better results. Third, the dated forward calls above, which are the same discipline applied to the things we write about every week.

This will take years rather than months, and we are saying so before it starts. When results exist they go on this page whatever they show. The aim is simple: when we give an opinion on a company, a technology or the economy, you should be able to see the record of how often that way of judging has been right, and weigh it accordingly.
